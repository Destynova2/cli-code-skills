# Sources and Evidence

Evidence levels: **[S]** strong (meta-analysis or replicated), **[M]** moderate
(a few studies, often lab), **[A]** practitioner experience or anecdote.
A book can be excellent practice material without each gesture being tested.

## Practitioner frameworks

- Voss & Raz, *Never Split the Difference* (2016). Labels, mirrors, calibrated
  questions, Ackerman. **[A]**, some mechanisms backed below.
- Fisher, Ury & Patton, *Getting to Yes* (1981, 3rd ed. 2011). Interests over
  positions, objective criteria, BATNA. Backbone for repeated relationships. **[M]**
- Malhotra & Bazerman, *Negotiation Genius* (2007). Preparation, biases, the
  "irrational" counterpart as missing information. **[M]**
- Malhotra, *Negotiating the Impossible* (2016). Reframe, negotiate the
  process before the substance. **[A/M]**
- Malhotra, "15 Rules for Negotiating a Job Offer", HBR (2014). Best short
  read on salary: likeability, their constraints, all issues together. **[M]**
- Diamond, *Getting More* (2010). Incremental steps, the other side's own
  standards. **[A]**
- Lax & Sebenius, *3-D Negotiation* (2006). Set up the table: who really
  decides, in what order. **[M]**
- Thompson, *The Mind and the Heart of the Negotiator*. Evidence-based
  textbook: fixed-pie bias, logrolling, compatible issues. **[S]**
- Cialdini, *Influence* (rev. 2021). Read the other side's tactics. **[S]**
  for most effects.
- Grant, *Give and Take* (2013). Cooperate without becoming the one who
  absorbs every free request. **[M]**
- Meyer, *The Culture Map* (2014). France as principles-first,
  confrontational in debate, hierarchical. **[M]**, use as a hint, not a
  stereotype.
- Harvard Program on Negotiation (pon.harvard.edu): free articles on BATNA,
  first offers, email negotiation, MESO.

## Practice material

- Black Swan Group mirroring and labeling demos (YouTube): pause after the
  counterpart's answer, improvise a French follow-up, compare.
- Malhotra, HBS, *How to Negotiate Your Job Offer* (video): rank three
  components of an offer, prepare one grouped ask the other side can defend
  internally.
- Duarte, *The secret structure of great talks*: pitch in one minute the
  current state and what concretely changes after the decision.
- Reddit cases to replay in `sim` (figures belong to their authors): TJM
  renewal via an ESN (r/developpeurs), budget too small for the quote
  (r/smallbusiness, play a total-envelope buyer then a per-unit-cap buyer),
  IT project refused by finance (r/sysadmin, replay the committee).

## Research

- Tversky & Kahneman (1974): anchoring. **[S]**
- Northcraft & Neale (1987): experts anchored by list price and deny it. **[S]**
- Galinsky & Mussweiler (2001): first offers as anchors; counter by focusing
  on the other side's reservation point or your own target. **[S]**
- Petrowsky, Loschelder et al. (2025), OBHDP 191, meta-analysis of 90
  studies plus two preregistered experiments: first-mover advantage, and
  ambitious openers improve the outcome when a deal closes, but bring more
  impasses and a worse experience for the receiver. Both effects weaken as
  the number of issues grows. Weigh the cost of failure. **[S]**
- Mason, Lee, Wiley & Ames (2013): precise offers are potent anchors. **[M]**
  Loschelder et al. (2016): excessive precision can backfire with experts. **[M]**
- Ames & Mason (2015): tandem anchoring with ranges. **[M]**
- Medvec & Galinsky (2005): MESO improve outcome and satisfaction. **[M]**
- Leonardelli et al. (2019), six experiments: MESOs vs a single offer, effects
  on joint outcome and perceived cooperation. **[M]**
- Curhan et al.: silence and pauses linked to value creation in bilateral
  negotiation; relative status matters in some conditions. Leave thinking
  time, no fixed seconds. **[M]**
- Maddux, Mullen & Galinsky (2008): behavioural mimicry increases deals. **[M]**
- Lieberman et al. (2007): labeling one's *own* affect lowers amygdala
  response. Indirect support only for labeling the counterpart. **[M]**
- Galinsky, Maddux, Gilin & White (2008): perspective-taking beats empathy
  for outcomes. **[M]**
- Brooks & Schweitzer (2011): anxiety lowers first offers and speeds exit.
  Brooks (2014): reframe anxiety as excitement. **[M]**
- Van Kleef, De Dreu & Manstead (2004): displayed anger extracts concessions.
  Sinaceur & Tiedens (2006): mostly from parties with poor alternatives. It
  costs the relationship. **[M]**
- Bowles, Babcock & Lai (2007): backlash against women who negotiate. **[S/M]**
  Bowles & Babcock (2013): relational accounts reduce it. **[M]**
- Morris et al. (2002); Thompson & Nadler (2002): email negotiation brings more
  impasses and hostile attributions; a quick informal contact first helps. **[M]**
- Jäckel, Zerres & Hüffmeier (2024): active listening raises joint gains after
  a multi-issue offer; no general effect shown elsewhere. **[M]**
- Bond & DePaulo (2006): lie detection near chance (~54 %). **[S]**

## Known critiques of Voss

1. Context bias: hostage talks are one-shot and extreme; most work deals are
   repeated games where a spotted tactic costs trust.
2. Caricature of *Getting to Yes*: labels and calibrated questions are
   interest-discovery tools, the core of Fisher & Ury. Complementary.
3. Anecdotal evidence, survivorship bias: failures are not reported.
4. Mehrabian's 7-38-55 rule is misused: it covered single words with
   incongruent tone, not communication in general.
5. "Let them anchor first" conflicts with first-offer research when you hold
   good data.
6. Ackerman is distributive haggling; it hurts in hiring and hierarchical
   relationships.
7. No-oriented questions often sound sarcastic in French.
8. Lie-detection cues (Pinocchio, rule of three as detector) are fragile.
9. "Never split the difference" is too absolute: on low stakes, splitting can
   be rational when negotiating costs more than the gap.
